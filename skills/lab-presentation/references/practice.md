# Presentation practice

What published work says about talks and slides, kept only where it changes what you do. Open
this page when you plan the outline or the notes, or when the user questions a slide rule.

Every item comes from the research note `docs/research/lab-presentation.md`, gathered on
2026-11-09. That note has the full reports, the effect sizes and the open points. A source
marked *(abstract)* was read from its abstract only. "The retro" is what the lab learned from
building its first decks with an agent.

Most of the experiments used students who watched short recorded lessons. None tested experts
at a live seminar. Treat an item as a default, and say so when the user asks how sure it is.

## The talk

| Do this | How established | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open with the need, the task and the main message, then a preview. Order the body as points that support the message, not as the history of the work. Give methods only where they help convince. End with a review and the conclusion. | Expert opinion | Doumont and co-authors, *English Communication for Scientists*, units 4.1 and 4.5, Nature Education |
| Start from about one slide per minute, one idea per slide and three points to keep. Cut anything the talk will not discuss. | Expert opinion, no data | Bourne 2007, *PLoS Comput Biol*, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.0030077; Naegle 2021, *PLoS Comput Biol*, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009554 |
| In a five-minute talk: one story, methods in two sentences at most, start closing at about 80% of the time, and repeat the key message once with a different picture. | Expert opinion | Lortie 2017, *PLoS Comput Biol*, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005373 |
| Break a long explanation into segments that each mean something, and mark what to look at. | Meta-analyses | Rey 2019, *Educ Psychol Rev*, doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4 *(abstract)*; Noetel 2022, *Rev Educ Res*, doi:10.3102/00346543211052329 *(abstract)* |
| Cut material that is interesting but beside the point. | Meta-analysis; a survey of 205 audience members named it the most common and most annoying fault | Sundararajan and Adesope 2020, *Educ Psychol Rev*, doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4 *(abstract)*; Kosslyn 2012, *Front Psychol*, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00230 |

## The words on a slide

| Do this | How established | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Write the title as a sentence that states the slide's claim, two lines at most, and fill the body with visual evidence. | One controlled study, by the method's authors. Title, word count and graphics changed together, so the title's share is unknown | Garner and Alley 2013, *Int J Eng Educ* 29(6):1564 |
| Put the fact to remember in the title, not in a bullet. | Weak: not randomised, and one assessment calls the gains modest | Alley 2006, *Tech Commun* 53(2):225; Farkas 2010, unpublished assessment |
| Never put the spoken sentences on the slide. Keep labels of two or three words beside the part of the figure they describe. | Controlled studies on students | Mayer and Johnson 2008, *J Educ Psychol* 100(2):380 *(abstract)*; Yue 2013, *J Educ Psychol*, doi:10.1037/a0031971 *(abstract)* |
| Do not strip a slide to no words. Keep the key terms, above all for newcomers and for slides with no figure. | Meta-analysis of 57 studies | Adesope and Nesbit 2012, *J Educ Psychol*, doi:10.1037/a0026147 *(abstract)*; Liu 2015, *Comput Educ*, doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2015.04.014 *(abstract)* |
| If a point matters, put it on the slide in short form. A dense slide makes the audience miss what is only said. | One controlled study | Wecker 2012, *Comput Educ*, doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2012.01.013 *(abstract)* |
| Explain a figure aloud, not in a paragraph beside it. For a deck read alone, write the words down. | Meta-analysis; the result reverses when readers set the pace | Ginns 2005, *Learn Instr*, doi:10.1016/j.learninstruc.2005.07.001 *(abstract)*; Tabbers 2004, *Br J Educ Psychol*, doi:10.1348/000709904322848824 *(abstract)* |
| Spell out any term or abbreviation that is not in the audience's daily use. | One experiment on written text; one count of abbreviations in papers | Bullock 2019, *Public Underst Sci*, doi:10.1177/0963662519865687 *(abstract)*; Barnett and Doubleday 2020, *eLife*, doi:10.7554/eLife.60080 |
| Write the notes as an outline, in other words than the slide's. | Expert opinion plus one study. No study of speaker notes was found | Doumont, as above; Yue 2013, as above |

## Size, colour, figures and animation

| Do this | How established | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size text for the farthest seat. 24 pt reaches about 6 screen heights and 20 pt about 4 to 5. A hall 8 screen heights deep needs more. | A display standard, read from its open summary, and an assumed letter shape. A guide, not a measurement | AVIXA, "Display Image Size for 2D Content in Audiovisual Systems"; Legge and Bigelow 2011, *J Vision*, doi:10.1167/11.5.8 |
| Treat text at or below 20 pt as a fault. | Expert rule; 65% of 140 sampled decks broke it. The number itself is untested | Kosslyn 2012, as above |
| Give Chinese text the same point floor as Latin, never a lower one. | Small controlled study of legibility thresholds | Zhang 2007, *Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci*, doi:10.1167/iovs.06-1195 |
| Keep text contrast at 4.5 to 1 or more. | A web standard, written for screens | WCAG 2.2, Understanding 1.4.3 |
| Take category colours from the eight-colour palette in slide rule 10. Use at most six to eight on a slide; past that, split into panels. | Expert design, widely adopted | Wong 2011, *Nat Methods*, doi:10.1038/nmeth.1618; Okabe and Ito 2008, <https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/> |
| Never encode by colour alone. Label each series on the data, with no separate legend. | Expert opinion | Okabe and Ito, as above |
| Redraw a paper figure for the slide: less detail, thicker lines, larger labels, no vertical text, no plot background. | Expert opinion | Rougier 2014, *PLoS Comput Biol*, doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003833 |
| Colour only the element the slide is about. With no reason for a colour, use black. | One controlled study, cited and not opened, plus expert opinion | Wong 2011, *Nat Methods*, doi:10.1038/nmeth.1762; Rougier 2014, as above |
| No looping movie, no moving emphasis, no decorative transition. | Expert opinion | Wong 2011, doi:10.1038/nmeth.1762; Kosslyn 2012, as above |
| At most four groups on a slide, four items in a list and two lines in a bullet. | A rule drawn from memory research; the cut-offs are expert opinion | Kosslyn 2012, as above |
| Do not choose a font by its serifs. | Small controlled study | Arditi and Cho 2005, *Vision Res*, doi:10.1016/j.visres.2005.06.013 *(abstract)* |

## Where the retro and the research disagree

- **Phrases, not sentences.** The retro said it of the whole slide. The research agrees for
  the body and disagrees for the title: three sources want a sentence there. Slide rules 1 and
  2 follow the research.
- **Less text is better.** The retro asked for less text, and that is not always true. Key terms on the slide
  help listeners who know little and slides with no figure. Slide rule 1 keeps the key terms.
- **The size floor.** The retro used 18 pt. The research finds 18 pt readable only from about
  3 to 5 screen heights away, and an expert rule counts 20 pt or below as a fault. Slide rule
  8 uses 20 pt, and 24 pt in a hall.
- **Reveal by section.** The retro supports it. The research is contested: one lab found lower
  scores after step-by-step entrances than after static slides (Mahar 2009, *Inf Syst Educ J*
  7(82), direction read from the abstract), and one expert rule counts a list shown all at
  once as a fault (Kosslyn 2012). Nothing tested a reveal by section. Slide rule 12 follows
  the retro.
- **What the audience likes.** The retro has no item on it, and the research warns against
  it as a guide: in two studies the group that learned more preferred the worse design. Do
  not weaken a rule because a slide looks empty.

## Not settled

- Talk length: the sources cover five minutes and one slide per minute. Nothing separates a
  15-minute talk from a 50-minute one.
- Chinese slides: no credible source was found. The size floor is the only item with evidence.
- Projected contrast under room light, whitespace and label sizes for slide figures: nothing
  measured. Apply the text floor to axis labels.
